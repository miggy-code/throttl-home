Most AI pilots begin with the easiest number to collect:

**How much faster did the task become?**

That number matters. It is also an easy way to fool yourself.

A draft can take less time to produce and more time to check. A classifier can move work faster into the wrong queue. A customer response can be ready sooner and still create another conversation when someone has to fix it.

The useful question is what happened after the AI produced its output.

## The research is more mixed than the headlines

There is good evidence that AI assistance can improve performance in some settings.

A [National Bureau of Economic Research study](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161) of 5,179 customer-support agents reported a 14% average increase in issues resolved per hour after a generative AI assistant was introduced. The gains were larger for novice and lower-skilled workers. The impact was minimal for experienced and highly skilled workers.

That is a meaningful result. It is also a result from one workflow, one tool, one workforce, and one operating environment. It does not tell an accounting firm, manufacturer, staffing company, or logistics operator what to expect from its own pilot.

Other evidence points the other way. [METR reported](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/) that AI-allowed tasks took 19% longer in a specific randomized study of experienced open-source developers. That does not mean AI generally slows work. It does mean that a universal productivity multiplier is a poor way to think about the problem.

The result depends on the task, the user, the surrounding process, and what happens after the output is generated.

## Start with the complete workflow

Before adding AI, name the workflow you are changing.

“We want to use AI across the business” is not a workflow.

“We want to reduce the time from an inbound service request to a reviewed response without increasing corrections or customer escalations” is one.

That definition gives you something to measure. It also gives you a boundary.

A useful workflow map includes the full episode, from intake to downstream result.

The review boundary sits inside that chain. Two questions determine how much oversight the workflow needs:

1. How serious is the consequence of an error?
2. How difficult is it to reverse the action?

The AI step is one part of the chain. A pilot that measures only the AI step is measuring the easiest part, not necessarily the important part.

Start with four measurement layers:

The details behind those layers matter:

- **Task:** minutes per completed case, throughput, queue age, and completion rate.
- **Quality:** first-pass acceptance, correction rate, rework minutes, missed exceptions, and severe-error count.
- **Downstream:** resolution quality, customer response or complaint signal, conversion or retention, margin or cash collection, defects or missed deadlines, and on-time completion.
- **Burden and downside:** reviewer minutes, escalations, duplicate data entry, user workarounds, privacy or security exceptions, customer recovery work, and tool or maintenance cost.

The right downstream measure depends on the workflow. A customer-support team may track resolution and sentiment. A manufacturer may track defects, rework, or downtime. A professional-services firm may track client corrections, matter cycle time, or missed issues.

The method can travel. The metric cannot be copied without thinking about the work.

## Human review is a design question

“Human in the loop” is too vague to be useful on its own.

Who reviews the output? What do they check? Can they reject it? Do they have the time and authority to intervene? What happens when the output is wrong? Can the action be reversed after release?

A practical review boundary starts with two questions:

<ReviewMatrix />

For low-consequence, highly reversible work, sampling and spot checks may be reasonable.

For moderate-consequence work, a named reviewer may need to approve the output before it reaches a customer or changes a system of record.

For high-consequence or difficult-to-reverse work, AI should not act independently. A qualified human should own the decision, review the evidence, and leave an accountable record.

This is an operating recommendation, not a legal classification. The right boundary depends on the business, the data, the people affected, the jurisdiction, and the workflow.

It also needs testing. A reviewer who lacks context, time, or authority is not much of a control. Review time and missed errors belong in the scorecard.

## Keep a small exception record

You do not need an enterprise governance department to learn from a pilot. You do need enough information to reconstruct what happened.

For sampled or exceptional cases, record:

```text
Experiment ID
Case ID
Workflow step
Input or source reference
Model or tool version
Output reference
Human decision: accepted, edited, rejected, or escalated
Reviewer
Exception category
Severity
Downstream result
Review time
Corrective action
Rollback or follow-up
```

Useful exception categories include:

- Unsupported claim
- Wrong or missing context
- Privacy or data-boundary concern
- Factual error
- Poor quality or rework
- Wrong classification or routing
- Policy or legal concern
- Unsafe recommendation
- System or integration failure
- Unanticipated downstream effect

The point is not paperwork. The point is to see where the workflow leaves its reliable operating range.

A recurring exception may mean the model needs improvement. It may also mean the workflow is poorly defined, the source data is incomplete, the reviewer is overloaded, or the task should not be automated.

## Decide before the pilot becomes permanent

A pilot without a decision date tends to become informal production use.

Before launch, define what would cause you to:

- Stop
- Redesign
- Hold for more evidence
- Narrow the scope
- Scale the workflow
- Make the controls permanent

A simple operating rhythm can help.

### Day 7: Can we measure and control it?

Name the workflow, owner, users, data boundary, reviewer, baseline, primary outcome, and stop conditions.

If you cannot explain what changes and what stays constant, the pilot is not ready.

### Day 30: Is there evidence of net value?

Compare the AI-assisted workflow with the baseline or a documented comparison. Count review, rework, exceptions, and downstream results alongside task speed.

If the output is faster but the complete workflow is not better, the pilot has produced a useful answer.

### Day 90: Does the result hold?

Check whether the benefit remains after the novelty and extra pilot attention decline. Review rare but serious failures. Recalculate the actual operating cost. Confirm that the fallback process works.

Then choose stop, redesign, hold, narrow-scale, or institutionalize.

The 7/30/90 cadence is a practical recommendation, not a universal research standard. Low-volume or high-consequence workflows may need a different design. If the workflow has too few cases for a confident conclusion, report the uncertainty instead of forcing a pass or fail.

## The best result may be “not yet”

A good AI pilot does not have to end in expansion.

It may show that the workflow is too irregular. The data may be too difficult to control. Review may cost more than the time saved. The consequences of a wrong output may be too high. Or nobody may have clear responsibility for the exceptions.

Those are useful findings.

The point of a pilot is not to prove that AI belongs in the workflow. The point is to find out whether the workflow can produce acceptable value with acceptable risk, using a process the team can maintain.

## A practical starting point

Choose one recurring workflow with:

- A stable intake
- A visible bottleneck
- A named owner
- A measurable downstream outcome
- Mostly reversible actions
- Enough volume to observe what happens

Run the manual process long enough to establish a baseline. Introduce AI in assistive, draft, or recommendation-only mode. Keep the old process available. Log the exceptions. Review the result at a fixed date.

Do not begin by asking where to add AI.

Begin with this:

> Which workflow is worth changing, and what evidence would tell us to stop?

If you are testing AI in one recurring workflow, a useful first step is to map what happens before and after the AI output. A private education conversation can help clarify the workflow, measures, review boundary, and next decision without assuming the answer is automation.
